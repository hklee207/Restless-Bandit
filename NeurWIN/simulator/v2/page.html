Learning which car to charge

A charging station can serve only a few cars at once. We replicate a neural method that learns a priority score for every car, check it against the exact answer, and find where the learned score beats the textbook rule and where it loses.

Key findings

  1. The result reproduces. With the authors' own code, NeurWIN's reward climbs from −301.0 to −99.3 and passes the exact Whittle index (−105.1) after about 570 training episodes. The paper reports about 600. A second machine gave the same numbers.
  2. The learned score beats the exact one when the station is small. With 4 cars and 1 charger, NeurWIN wins in all 50 test runs. The two policies pick different cars in 40% of rounds, mostly because NeurWIN charges a car that will run late before it becomes urgent.
  3. The advantage fades as the station grows. It shrinks from +5.5% (4 cars) to +1.7% (20 cars) and reverses at 100 cars, where the exact index wins by 2.0%. This fits the theory: the Whittle index is guaranteed to be near-optimal only for large systems.

01Why this example

Our research asks how to spend a limited amount of oversight across many AI agents that run at the same time. Only a few agents can be checked each period, and every agent's risk keeps changing whether or not it is checked. In operations research this is a restless multi-armed bandit: many "arms," a budget of M activations per round, and arms that evolve on their own.

The classic solution is the Whittle index, a priority score computed for each arm from its current state. Each round you activate the M arms with the highest scores. The catch is that the exact score is known for very few problems. NeurWIN (Nakhleh et al., 2021) trains a small neural network to produce the score instead.

Before using NeurWIN on agents, we replicated one of its experiments. We picked EV charging because it is one of the rare cases where the exact Whittle index is known in closed form, so the learned score can be checked against the right answer.

AnalogyA triage nurse with one specialist and a full waiting room gives each patient a number and sends in the highest one. The research question is how to compute that number.

02The charging problem

A station has N charging spots but enough power for only M cars per round. When a spot is free, a new car may pull in (70% chance each round). On arrival it announces how many rounds until it leaves, D (1 to 12), and how many units of charge it needs, B (1 to 9).

The station earns 1 − c = 0.5 for every unit it delivers. If a car leaves before it is full, the station pays a penalty of 0.2 × B² on the charge still missing. Because the penalty grows with the square, a car that is 3 units short costs 1.8, nine times as much as a car that is 1 unit short. Each spot is one arm, and its state is the pair (D, B). Activating an arm for a round charges one unit; the reward is r = 1 − c = 0.5 per unit, and a vehicle that leaves B units short costs F(B) = 0.2 B².

03Algorithm 1, step by step

NeurWIN's network fθ takes an arm's state (D, B) and outputs one number. It never sees the full station during training. Algorithm 1 trains it on a single arm that pays an activation cost λ every round it is activated, instead of competing with other vehicles for the chargers.

Why does this produce the Whittle index? In Env(λ), the arm earns its reward minus λ each time it is activated. A state's Whittle index is the activation cost at which activating and staying passive are equally good. fθ is Whittle-accurate (Def. 4) if, for every λ, activating is optimal in Env(λ) exactly when fθ(s) > λ. A hard "activate iff fθ(s) > λ" rule has no gradient. So training plays Env*(λ), where the arm is activated with probability σm(fθ(s) − λ) (Sec. 4.2). By Thm. 1, a θ that does well in Env*(λ) for every λ gives a Whittle-accurate fθ as the sensitivity m grows.

04Watch both policies run

Below are two copies of the same station. They receive exactly the same cars at the same times. The left one charges by the exact Whittle index, the right one by NeurWIN. Each round you see the cars each policy is about to charge and why.

Watch for the moment the two disagree. The usual pattern is that the exact index charges the car that is leaving now, while NeurWIN charges a car that is a few rounds out but cannot finish on time either. With one charger, two late cars arriving at their last round together is the expensive event, and NeurWIN avoids it.

05Results

The replication

We ran the authors' training and test scripts with two one-line compatibility fixes and the paper's settings (sensitivity m = 1, 2,000 episodes = 400 mini-batches of R = 5, training seed 50). NeurWIN starts at −301.0, reaches −102.3 by episode 600 and ends at −99.3. The exact index scores −105.1. NeurWIN first matches it at the 570-episode checkpoint; the paper reports about 600. A rerun from scratch on a second machine matched every number within 0.05.

Why NeurWIN beats the "optimal" index

The gap is real: NeurWIN is better in all 50 test runs, by 5.8 on average (paired standard error 0.35). It is not caused by how the index breaks ties; three other tie-breaking rules move the index's score by less than 0.5. The policies differ in 40% of rounds (5,983 of 15,000), and Figure 3 shows the typical case.

The Whittle index is derived one spot at a time, as if that spot could buy charging in any future round. That assumption is close to true when there are many spots and the collisions average out. With 4 cars and 1 charger it is not, and NeurWIN's imperfect score happens to lean toward charging earlier. To test this explanation we reused the same trained network at larger sizes, keeping one charger per four spots.

Table 1. Mean discounted reward over 50 paired runs. Paired SE = standard error of the per-run difference.

One caution on the largest case: the network was trained for 2,000 episodes, while the authors' default is 30,000. Part of the loss at N = 100 may come from under-training rather than from the theory.

06What this means for monitoring AI agents

The same structure fits our problem. Each running agent is a spot, each monitor check is a unit of charge, and the score would be computed from the agent's current context. The mapping below is our proposal and has not been tested.

What the replication suggests

Monitoring fleets may be small, and at small sizes a learned score can beat the textbook index. Sharing one scorer across agents needs agents that behave alike and independently; mixed models or shared memory would break that. The open questions we want to discuss:

  1. Is beating the Whittle index at small N a known effect, and does it matter for small agent fleets?
  2. The code replays the same car arrivals in all 5 episodes of a mini-batch, which Algorithm 1 doesn't mention. Is that a deliberate variance-reduction choice?
  3. For mixed-model fleets: one score per model, or the model's identity as part of the state?
  4. What compressed summary of an agent's memory should play the role of (D, B)?
  5. Algorithm 1 sets the activation cost λ = fθ(s0) from a random state s0. How would we sample s0 when the state is an agent's context?

07Methods and provenance

  • Code: authors' repository [2] with two compatibility fixes (zero_grad(set_to_none=False); integer seeds) and the paper's settings. Algorithm, environment and hyperparameters unchanged.
  • Training (Algorithm 1): 2,000 episodes of 300 rounds, mini-batches of R = 5, learning rate L = 0.001, discount β = 0.99, sensitivity m = 1, seed 50. Test: 50 runs, seed 30, a checkpoint every 10 episodes.
  • Verified twice: cloud run (Python 3.13) and a fresh rerun on the author's MacBook (Python 3.14), all numbers within 0.05. Repository hklee207/Restless-Bandit, branch dev, commit 1f81a21.
  • Limits: one training seed, as in the paper. The explanation for the small-N advantage is a hypothesis consistent with Figure 4, not a proof.
  • Section 03 shows values from replication/algorithm1.py, a line-by-line restatement of the authors' training loop in the paper's notation; with the same seeds it reproduces the authors' checkpoints exactly (max difference 0 after 10 and 2,000 episodes).
  • This page's simulator is a JavaScript port of deadlineSchedulingEnv.py, checked against the Python environment on three reference runs (identical rewards and choices for both policies).
